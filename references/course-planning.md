# Course planning

Read before creating or revising a curriculum, preparing module homework or a boundary project/exam, deciding completion, starting or extending a project, or adapting tasks to a subject. For task criteria and evidence judgments, use [Fair tasks and learning evidence](../SKILL.md#fair-tasks-and-learning-evidence); preserve checkpoints under [Portable record and storage](progress.md#portable-record-and-storage).

## Scope and structure

Organize **Course → Week → Section → Module → Concept → Mini-concept**. When setup details are omitted, state the assumptions and plan from beginner foundations toward comprehensive subject coverage at **20 hours per week**. Use a configurable 16-week starting estimate; determine the needed duration from the content and workload. Honor the learner's stated background, goals, hours, duration, and narrower scope.

For full weekly courses, use at least **3 sections per week** and **5 modules per section**. Vary the actual number of sections between weeks and modules between sections according to coherent outcomes and prerequisites. The minimums are floors, not a repeated template; choose meaningful topic boundaries rather than arbitrary variation. Derive the total module count from the plan. Explicit learner requests for different counts or a short, focused course override these defaults.

Fit instruction, guided and independent practice, homework, projects, review, and assessments together within the available hours. Check both module depth and the complete weekly budget before accepting a plan. If depth, count minimums, coverage, and available time conflict, explain the tradeoff and agree on a change to duration, scope, hours, or counts before finalizing. Preserve teaching depth rather than silently shortening modules or adding filler. Weeks are planned learning blocks, not evidence of mastery.

## Module depth

Each full-course module must have **at least 4 meaningful, ordered instructional parts**, usually 4–8, with more when its outcomes require them. Each part develops a distinct concept or skill needed for the module's outcome; together they provide enough explanation, application, and relevant failure cases or limitations to support its homework. A part count alone does not establish depth. Examples, images, checks, recaps, and homework support instruction and do not count as extra parts. Merge thin fragments into coherent modules instead of splitting topics merely to meet structural minimums.

Keep teaching responses small: deliver one manageable mini-concept at a time under [Lesson rhythm](../SKILL.md#lesson-rhythm), expanding a planned part across multiple turns when needed. A substantial module is a sequence of teaching steps, not one long response. Present module homework after the instructional parts; retain the ordinary checks between parts.

A learner-requested micro-lesson or narrow refresher may use fewer parts with its limited scope stated explicitly. Apply the full-course depth requirement to new or regrouped modules; preserve other approved modules and pending work unless the learner requests their revision.

## Plan contents and presentation

Plan every unit with:

- Course: goal, intended outcomes, audience, prerequisites, duration/workload, completion criteria, sources and coverage.
- Week: purpose, ordered sections, prerequisites, workload, cumulative review, final scope, and project slots.
- Section: purpose, outcomes, prerequisites, ordered modules, midterm scope, project alignment.
- Module: observable outcomes, prerequisites, ordered instructional parts, time estimate including practice and homework, homework intent, and mastery criteria.

Use stable IDs. Changing a title does not change the meaning of an existing objective. Display a compact overview unless the learner asks for the complete curriculum. For that request, show every week, section, and module inline in course order; a file supplements the response rather than replacing it. Include each module's identifier, title, outcomes, prerequisites, ordered instructional parts, time, and homework intent, plus assessment/project plans at their actual boundaries. If response limits require installments, label their coverage and retain the remaining position without calling a partial outline complete. Save the full plan where authorized.

Before accepting the plan, check every module for substantive instructional parts, every full-course week and section for the applicable minimums, actual variation in counts, and every week's total workload. Trace the concepts and operations needed by outcomes, homework, projects, and exams back to earlier planned instruction. Check their meaning as well as their prerequisite IDs; a structurally valid plan can still assess an untaught operation.

When browsing is available, compare suitable public university syllabi and established paid/free course outlines. Evaluate topic coverage, prerequisite order, current accuracy, exercises, projects, and assessment rather than claiming an objectively best course. Use authoritative subject references to check omissions. Distinguish a public syllabus from access to paid content; disclose inaccessible sources. If fresh research is unavailable, label the plan provisional. Create original teaching and assessment material.

Generate actual explanations, examples, exercises, homework prompts, project briefs, and assessment items only when the module or relevant review/assessment is active. Future modules remain plans. Expand active content progressively using current evidence; do not write a semester of lectures at the outset.

## Revising an approved plan

When restructuring is requested, map old objectives and instructional parts to their new locations. Preserve coverage, prerequisite order, agreed workload, original assessment criteria, learner evidence, and exact pending work. Retain existing counts outside the requested revision's scope; new-course count defaults do not authorize expanding an approved plan. An increase in module count describes regrouping, not added teaching depth. Identify any proposed coverage change separately.

Redistribute existing homework and section-assessment time across the new boundaries without multiplying the total workload. Preserve project scope and place each project after its actual prerequisites; structural regrouping alone does not create extra project slots. Recheck module depth and weekly budgets after remapping. Retain unmet requirements and historical completion evidence without treating new headings as new mastery.

## Assessments and completion

For new full-course plans, the configurable pattern is homework after every module, a project slot after two or three modules counted across section boundaries, a section midterm, and a weekly final. Allocate section-assessment time in proportion to scope and include all these activities, review, and debugging in the workload. Adjust task size and project spacing to fit that budget; for an existing plan, use [Revising an approved plan](#revising-an-approved-plan).

Ordinary teaching and practice receive immediate feedback. Midterms/finals default to a short portion without references followed by practical work allowing notes and documentation. State permitted tools before the attempt, including whether answer-generating assistance is allowed. A learner can choose different conditions. Use the normal one-task turn unless the learner requested a batch. Acknowledge receipt neutrally and defer detailed exam feedback until the end. Preserve original criteria and exact submissions. Help is available; mark the affected item assisted and use a fresh independent item later when needed.

A flexible threshold for important outcomes is two varied independent successes including application, adjusted with a stated rationale. Do not invent a universal numerical mastery score.

Module completion requires its required outcomes and homework. Section completion requires its modules, midterm, and assigned project slots. Week completion requires its sections and final, covering all scheduled integration work. A passing overall score cannot hide a missing essential objective. Guided project completion is useful but does not automatically prove independent competence. Map overlapping evidence explicitly; do not double-count a revealed answer. Reassess only the missing objectives after support. Delayed retention is not a gate on first advancement.

## Continuing projects

Begin with small projects. After readiness supports a continuing project, repeat three small projects followed by a continuing-project milestone; the milestone replaces that slot's small project. Plan steps together initially, then let the learner attempt them. Fade guidance as actual independent work supports it.

Keep a project checkpoint with stable identity, actual files/artifacts and version, working features, earlier decisions, unfinished work/defects, previous verification, and next milestone criteria. Inspect it and available work before every return. Explain the extension, preserve earlier functionality, and check for regressions. Extend that same project; report only progress supported by the checkpoint and available artifacts. If artifacts are missing, ask for the minimum needed context while preserving known facts.

## Subject adaptation

- **Programming:** distinguish reading output, writing code, executing supplied code, debugging, and design. Teach tools before assessing their use; verify relevant inputs where execution exists. Investigate warnings and errors rather than treating them as grading failures or automatically disabling safeguards.
- **Mathematics:** connect meaning to notation; explain why each transformation is valid. Distinguish slips from conceptual gaps. Accept alternative methods and assess proof only against taught assumptions and requirements.
- **Philosophy:** distinguish an author's position from interpretation. Evaluate argument reconstruction, premises, inference, support, objections, and replies. Preserve defensible disagreement.
- **English/literature/writing:** clarify language learning versus composition or literary study where necessary. Ground interpretation in text; disclose audience, purpose, genre, and rubric. Separate mechanics from optional style and preserve the learner's voice.
- **Other subjects:** choose observable evidence suited to the domain and acknowledge limits of remote evaluation. Do not force every project into a software application.

These profiles are starting guidance, not claims of completed human validation.
