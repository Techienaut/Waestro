# Course planning

Read before creating or revising a curriculum, preparing module homework or a boundary project/exam, deciding completion, starting or extending a project, or adapting tasks to a subject. For task criteria and evidence judgments, use [Fair tasks and learning evidence](../SKILL.md#fair-tasks-and-learning-evidence); preserve checkpoints under [Portable record and storage](progress.md#portable-record-and-storage).

## Scope and structure

Organize **Course → Week → Section → Module → Concept → Mini-concept**. Use the learner's goals, background, and requested scope. If setup is skipped, state the assumptions and plan from beginner foundations toward comprehensive coverage. Fix the weekly study budget at the learner's stated hours, or **20 hours per week** when they supply none.

Build sections around related outcomes and modules around teachable skills and their prerequisites. Use as many modules as a section needs. Give a distinct operation its own module when it needs its own instruction and practice. Combine closely related skills when one task can exercise them together. Derive the section and module counts from this work. A week is a study budget. When a section spans weeks, keep its section and module numbers and show the exact continuation in each week’s schedule.

Estimate the time for instruction, reading, guided and independent practice, homework, projects, review, and assessments. Schedule each activity once and show each week's total within the fixed budget. Report the resulting duration and total study hours. Add weeks as needed to preserve scope and depth, including beyond 16 weeks. If the learner also sets a deadline that cannot fit the requested coverage, show the hours needed and seek agreement on a longer schedule or narrower scope. Change the weekly hours only at the learner's request.

## Research and coverage

For a new course, research relevant roadmaps, university syllabi, established paid and free online courses (including MOOCs), and textbooks. Inspect the original roadmap, instructor or provider syllabus, and publisher or author table of contents. Where available, inspect learning objectives, lessons, problem sets, projects, or exams to judge depth beyond the outline. Compare authority, edition and currency, prerequisites, fit to the learner's goals, depth of instruction and practice, and assessment. Cite the named sources, link what you inspected, and explain your choices against these criteria. Limit any “best fit” claim to the learner's goals and the sources examined.

State the accessible level of detail for each source. A public syllabus can establish listed topics while leaving lesson or exercise depth unknown. If fresh sources are unavailable, use supplied material, state the limits, and label the plan provisional.

Use completionist coverage by default for a new full course. For a focused refresher, apply the learner’s narrower scope. Define “complete” against one or more named sources, such as the relevant roadmap.sh roadmap for a technical subject or a university syllabus. Honor the learner's named sources and give each source's version, edition or syllabus term (or access date for a changing, unversioned roadmap). Identify which sources the course covers in full. Identify supplemental sources separately and name the selected units or exercises they contribute.

1. Inventory each full-coverage source down to its published roadmap nodes and subtopics, syllabus units and outcomes, or textbook chapters and subsections. Retain the source's numbering or topic names, including minor operations and edge cases. Keep optional branches identifiable.
2. Show a coverage map with each required item, its source location, numbered teaching module, and planned practice or assessment that exercises the item. Several items may share an activity when it genuinely uses each skill. Identify the expected depth, including topics the learner will only survey, and mark inaccessible or unresolved items explicitly.
3. Reconcile the map against each source inventory before asking for curriculum approval. Report the number of required items mapped at the promised depth against the total for each source, where that total is verifiable. Identify any missing, survey-only, excluded, or unverifiable coverage and what would close each gap. Claim full *planned* coverage of a source only when every required item has a teaching module and practice or assessment at the promised depth. Track actual learner achievement through the ordinary evidence rules.

## Module depth

Give each module enough ordered instructional parts to reach its outcome. Each part develops a distinct concept or skill. Together they prepare the learner to apply the material, handle relevant failure cases or limitations, and attempt the homework. Examples, images, checks, recaps, and homework support those parts. Use a focused task to check a narrow outcome and allow a broader outcome the teaching and practice it requires.

Keep teaching responses small: deliver one manageable mini-concept at a time under [Lesson rhythm](../SKILL.md#lesson-rhythm), expanding a planned part across multiple turns when needed. A substantial module is a sequence of teaching steps, not one long response. Present module homework after the instructional parts; retain the ordinary checks between parts.

A learner-requested micro-lesson or narrow refresher can focus on a single outcome. Apply these depth checks to new or regrouped modules. Preserve other approved modules and pending work unless the learner requests their revision.

## Plan contents and presentation

Plan every unit with:

- Course: goal, intended outcomes, audience, prerequisites, duration/workload, completion criteria, sources and coverage.
- Week: purpose, ordered sections, prerequisites, workload, cumulative review, final scope, and project slots.
- Section: purpose, outcomes, prerequisites, ordered modules, midterm scope, project alignment.
- Module: observable outcomes, prerequisites, ordered instructional parts, time estimate including practice and homework, homework intent, and mastery criteria.

For a new course, show every numbered week and section in course order, with a separate entry for each module under its section. Give each module a stable number and title (for example, `Module 2.1.3 — Validate a CSV header`). Keep its identifier when renaming the same objective. Include each module's outcomes, prerequisites, ordered instructional parts, time, homework intent, and mastery criteria. Place project and assessment plans at their actual boundaries.

Present the full curriculum in chat by default. When authorized, also save the full plan. If a response limit requires installments, label each installment with the modules it covers, end at a module boundary, and identify the exact next entry. On hosts that require another learner turn, invite “continue” and resume at that entry. Keep the proposal open through the final installment, then follow [Start or resume](../SKILL.md#start-or-resume) for approval.

Before presenting the plan, check each module's instructional depth and prerequisite order, the coverage promised to the learner, and the total time scheduled each week. Trace the concepts and operations needed by homework, projects, and exams to earlier instruction. Verify that those lessons teach the actual operation each task requires.

Generate original explanations, examples, exercises, homework prompts, project briefs, and assessment items only when the module or relevant review/assessment is active. Future modules remain plans. Expand active content progressively using current evidence; do not write a semester of lectures at the outset.

## Revising an approved plan

When restructuring is requested, map old objectives and instructional parts to their new locations. Preserve coverage, prerequisite order, agreed workload, original assessment criteria, learner evidence, and exact pending work. Retain existing counts outside the requested revision's scope. Update affected source mappings and identify any proposed coverage change separately.

Redistribute existing homework and section-assessment time across the new boundaries without multiplying the total workload. Preserve project scope and place each project after its actual prerequisites; structural regrouping alone does not create extra project slots. Recheck module depth and weekly budgets after remapping. Retain unmet requirements and historical completion evidence without treating new headings as new mastery.

## Assessments and completion

For new full-course plans, the configurable pattern is homework after every module, a project slot after two or three modules counted across section boundaries, a section midterm, and a weekly final. Allocate section-assessment time in proportion to scope and include all these activities, review, and debugging in the workload. Adjust task size and project spacing to fit that budget; for an existing plan, use [Revising an approved plan](#revising-an-approved-plan).

Ordinary teaching and practice receive immediate feedback. Midterms/finals default to a short portion without references followed by practical work allowing notes and documentation. State permitted tools before the attempt, including whether answer-generating assistance is allowed. A learner can choose different conditions. Use the normal one-task turn unless the learner requested a batch. Acknowledge receipt neutrally and defer detailed exam feedback until the end. Preserve original criteria and exact submissions. Help is available; mark the affected item assisted and use a fresh independent item later when needed.

A flexible threshold for important outcomes is two varied independent successes including application, adjusted with a stated rationale. Do not invent a universal numerical mastery score.

Module completion requires its required outcomes and homework. Section completion requires its modules, midterm, and assigned project slots. Week completion requires the outcomes, integration work and final scheduled for that week. A section spanning multiple weeks remains open through its last module and section assessment. A passing overall score cannot hide a missing essential objective. Guided project completion is useful but does not automatically prove independent competence. Map overlapping evidence explicitly; do not double-count a revealed answer. Reassess only the missing objectives after support. Delayed retention is not a gate on first advancement.

## Continuing projects

Begin with small projects. After readiness supports a continuing project, repeat three small projects followed by a continuing-project milestone; the milestone replaces that slot's small project. Plan steps together initially, then let the learner attempt them. Fade guidance as actual independent work supports it.

Keep a project checkpoint with stable identity, actual files/artifacts and version, working features, earlier decisions, unfinished work/defects, previous verification, and next milestone criteria. Inspect it and available work before every return. Explain the extension, preserve earlier functionality, and check for regressions. Extend that same project; report only progress supported by the checkpoint and available artifacts. If artifacts are missing, ask for the minimum needed context while preserving known facts.

## Subject adaptation

- **Programming:** distinguish reading output, writing code, executing supplied code, debugging, and design. Teach tools before assessing their use; verify relevant inputs where execution exists. Investigate warnings and errors rather than treating them as grading failures or automatically disabling safeguards.
- **Mathematics:** connect meaning to notation; explain why each transformation is valid. Distinguish slips from conceptual gaps. Accept alternative methods and assess proof only against taught assumptions and requirements.
- **Philosophy:** distinguish an author's position from interpretation. Evaluate argument reconstruction, premises, inference, support, objections, and replies. Preserve defensible disagreement.
- **English/literature/writing:** clarify language learning versus composition or literary study where necessary. Ground interpretation in text; disclose audience, purpose, genre, and rubric. Separate mechanics from optional style and preserve the learner's voice.
- **Other subjects:** choose observable evidence suited to the domain and acknowledge limits of remote evaluation. Do not force every project into a software application.

These profiles are starting guidance, not claims of completed human validation.
